Five shapes, one vocabulary

Last week the founder said "legal" and your hand hesitated. It knew the word mattered and had nothing to draw for it, so it drew a box, and the box cost nothing. Tonight the hesitation goes. Mike Rohde builds every icon in the Handbook from five shapes, and every noun that comes up in your meetings can be a recipe of two or three of them, learned once, drawn the same way every time. You leave with twenty of those recipes on one sheet, for your own work, and the founder's twelve ideas get their marks along the way.

Part 1 · covers the five elements

Circle, square, triangle, line, dot

5 min live

The Handbook's index lists "five basic drawing elements" at page 16 and again at pages 149 to 152, with an exercise at 154 and 155. They are a circle, a square, a triangle, a line and a dot. Nothing in the book is drawn from anything else, and nothing on this page is either.

LiveEverything is five shapes3 min▶

Rohde's claim is smaller and stronger than "anyone can draw". It is that everything you will need to draw in a meeting decomposes into five elements, and that the decomposition is the thing to learn. A bean is a circle with a line through it. A calendar is a square with three lines. A set of scales is a triangle, a line and two circles. Once you see the recipe you cannot unsee it, and the recipe is what your hand memorises.

  • Two or three elements per icon. A bean needs two. Scales need four and are already at the edge. Anything that needs six is a drawing, and a drawing is twenty seconds you are not listening.
  • The recipe, not the picture. Nobody remembers what their camera icon looks like; they remember "square, then circle". Written down as words, the recipe is portable and repeatable in a way a sketch never is.
  • People come from the same five. A circle and a line make a person. Rohde has two fuller methods for figures, the star method and the Gray method, at Handbook pages 156 to 161; they exist, they are his, and Part 3 shows how far a circle and a line get you without them.

The point of an alphabet is that it is finite. You do not learn to draw coffee; you learn five shapes and a habit of asking which two make a bean.

Self-studyTwelve, five, and why the number does not matter4 min▶

There is a second alphabet in circulation. Dave Gray's visual alphabet has twelve characters; XPLANE, the firm he founded, publishes it as a worksheet and credits it to him, and Sunni Brown's 2011 article corroborates the count with her line that "only twelve forms" make up the visual landscape. Twelve, per Gray, is a verified number.

What the twelve are called is not. Every fetched source shows the glyphs as an image with no caption, and the third-party lists that name them disagree with each other: one splits them as five plus nine, another is a twelve-item mnemonic whose own author says he does not know where it came from. So this page says twelve, credits Gray, prints no list of names, and teaches Rohde's five, which are indexed by page number in a book you can hold.

The argument about whether the alphabet has five letters or twelve is beside the point. Both are alphabets: a finite set of marks that combine into everything else. Once you accept that, the drawing objection has nowhere left to stand, because nobody says they cannot learn twelve letters. Pick either. Use one. What matters is that the set is closed and your hand has been through all of it.

Real world

Daybreak's ops lead cannot draw and says so in every introduction. In one lunch break she built nine icons for the nouns that recur in her week: roastery, supplier, sleeve, subscriber, email, legal, finance, support, checklist. Each is two or three of the five, written as a recipe next to the mark. She has used the same nine in every meeting since, and a colleague who borrows her notes now reads them without asking what anything means, because the truck has always been the supplier and the scales have always been legal.

Part 2 · covers the vocabulary

Your twenty nouns, your twenty marks

5 min live

The founder's talk has about a dozen recurring nouns. So does your Monday standup, and yours are not coffee. A vocabulary is the set of marks for the nouns that come up every week, built on a quiet afternoon so that in the meeting the hand does not have to invent anything.

LiveWhy the vocabulary is built before the meeting3 min▶

Session 1 put a price on drawing: twenty seconds on an icon is the batch size going past. Most of those twenty seconds are not spent drawing. They are spent deciding what to draw, and the deciding is the expensive part, because deciding what a legal icon should look like uses the same attention that was supposed to be listening to the founder. Session 3 measures that cost with a bench. Tonight removes it, by moving the deciding to an afternoon when nobody is talking.

The mark has to be in the hand already. When the founder says "legal", a hand with a vocabulary draws a triangle, a line and two circles in four seconds without consulting anyone, the way it writes the letter L. A hand without one draws a box, which is fine, or invents scales on the spot, which costs the next sentence.

Doug Neill's Verbal to Visual course has a module on exactly this, building a visual vocabulary before you need it, inside the imagery element of his nine-module course (module 4.2 on verbaltovisual.com). The shape of that idea, a personal set built in advance and kept where you can see it, is the influence on tonight. His icons are his; the ones on this page were drawn for it. The sheet lives in the front of the notebook, on the inside cover, where it is open before the speaker starts.

Self-studyFour rules for an icon that survives4 min▶

An icon survives if it is still being drawn, the same way, six months later, and still read correctly by someone who did not draw it. Four rules get most icons there.

  • Two or three elements, not five. Every element you add costs a second to draw and a second to decide. The scales are at four and are the most expensive thing on the sheet; the bean is at two and is nearly free.
  • Recognisable at thumbnail size. Draw it at the size of your thumbnail. If it needs to be bigger to be read, it has too much in it. Notes are dense, and the icon will sit next to capitals, not on a poster.
  • Drawn the same way every time. The recipe fixes the order: square, then circle, then the small square on top. Same order, same shape, so your hand stops thinking about it and a colleague sees the same camera on every page.
  • One per noun. If two nouns want the same icon, one of them gets a letter inside it. Email and sleeve share a recipe; the flap sits at the top of one and the bottom of the other, and if that ever confuses, the sleeve gets an S.

If the list stalls at twelve, the missing eight are usually people and problems rather than things: the team you always wait on, the report that is always late, the customer who always calls. Those recur harder than any system does, and they need marks more, because they are the ones you will want to find on the page next week.

Part 3 · covers faces and people

A person is a circle and a line

3 min live

People are the thing everyone is sure they cannot draw, and the thing notes need most, because half of what a meeting decides is who owns what. A circle and a line, plus an initial, does the job in five seconds. A face is three marks and one of them carries the whole mood.

LiveFaces in three marks2 min▶

A circle, two dots, one line. That is a face, and the angle of the line is the whole expression. Curve it up and the room was pleased; flat and the room was neutral; curve it down and finance was not happy about the margin. You do not draw eyes or hair or a nose. Two dots and a line read as a face from across a table, and they take three seconds.

People appear in notes to mark who said or owns a thing. Nobody needs a likeness of the founder. They need to know that the checklist has an owner and who it is, which is a stick figure with an initial next to its head. When the founder said nobody owned the checklist, the figure has a question mark instead of an initial, and that question mark is the most useful thing on the page a week later.

Posture is two lines. Arms up is a decision or a win. Arms down is waiting. One arm pointing at a thing is ownership. Beyond that you are drawing, and drawing is the expensive kind of listening.

Self-studyWhat to leave to the books3 min▶

Rohde's Handbook carries the exercises that turn a stick figure into a figure with weight and gesture: the people-drawing section at pages 156 to 161, with the star method and the Gray method, and the lettering ladder at pages 166 to 171, which runs from single-line through two-line and triple-line to block letters and drop shadows. Those are the paid core of the book. They are named here so you know where they live, and left there.

The Workbook goes further into craft: serif, slab serif, script, condensed, extended and 3D lettering at pages 180 to 190. That is for people who find they want the letters to be lovely, and some do. It is not required for a page that keeps twelve ideas, and every minute spent on a slab serif in a meeting is a minute of the meeting gone.

Session 5 takes exactly one rung of the lettering ladder, the difference between a single-line word and a heavier one, and uses it for hierarchy so that a colleague's eye lands on the right thing first. It stops there. Anyone who wants the rest of the ladder has two good books and knows the page numbers.

Build-along

Twenty icons in twenty minutes

★ 24 min · everyone builds

You brought twenty nouns. You leave with twenty recipes and twenty marks on one sheet, drawn twice. The rule for the whole twenty minutes is: write the recipe before you draw the mark, and no mark gets more than three elements on the first pass.

Lay out the sheet. Rule a four by five grid on one page, twenty slots, with the pen you will use in meetings. Write one noun in the top corner of each slot, all twenty, before any drawing happens. A slot with a noun and no mark is a promise; a mark with no noun is decoration.

Recipe first. Under each noun write which two or three of the five elements make it: "square + triangle", "circle + line". Do all twenty recipes before drawing any of them. Where two nouns get the same recipe, decide now which one carries a letter.

Draw each once, from its recipe, in the order the recipe says. Count the seconds under your breath as you go and write the number beside the mark. Do not fix anything. An ugly bean that took three seconds is finished.

Time the slow ones. Circle the three highest second-counts on the sheet. For each, remove one element from the recipe and redraw it in the margin. The scales lose a circle; the truck loses a wheel. If it still reads, the shorter recipe wins and replaces the long one.

Draw each again on a fresh page, from the recipes only, with the first sheet face down. Anything you cannot reproduce had a recipe that was really a picture; rewrite it as elements. The second sheet is the one that goes in the front of the notebook.

Rule the grid with the meeting pen. Four across, five down, lines light. A ruler is fine tonight; in a meeting the sheet is already made, so it never matters again.

Nouns first, all twenty, top corner of each slot. Nothing gets drawn until every slot has a name. This is the list you brought, in your own words, not the founder's.

Recipe under the noun, in words. "circle + line" takes two seconds to write and is what you will actually remember. The mark is the output of the recipe, not the other way round.

Draw once, count seconds, write the number. No erasing, no second attempt in the same slot. The number is the finding; the drawing is just evidence.

Star the three slowest and drop an element from each. Redraw in the margin. Keep whichever version still reads at thumbnail size with fewer marks.

Turn the page and redraw all twenty from the recipes. Anything that will not come back is a recipe to rewrite. Bring the second sheet to session 3; it is what you capture with.

If you take one thing from tonight. The sheet is not art. It is a keyboard: twenty keys your hand knows without looking, so that when the speaker says a noun the mark is already down and your ears are still on the next sentence. A keyboard nobody would frame is still the thing that lets you type without watching your fingers.

Homework

Before session 3

25 min
  1. Capture one real meeting using only the twenty icons plus capitals. No sentences, and no mark that is not on the sheet. If a thing comes up that has no icon, write its handle in capitals and draw a box, exactly as in session 1.
  2. Note which icon you needed and did not have, and add it to the sheet that evening, recipe first. Most people find two or three. That is the sheet growing toward your actual week rather than the one you imagined on Tuesday.
  3. Redraw the whole sheet once from the recipes, first sheet face down. Time yourself. Under seven minutes for twenty is the hand knowing them; over ten means three or four recipes are still pictures in disguise.
  4. Bring a laptop to session 3, because the bench plays the founder's talk at speaking pace and times what you keep. The icons you built tonight are what make a fast capture possible, and the bench will show what the slow ones cost.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why is the icon vocabulary built on a quiet afternoon rather than invented during the meeting?

Session 1 priced a rendered icon at twenty seconds, and most of that is deciding, not drawing. Deciding uses the attention that was meant for the speaker. A vocabulary moves the deciding to an afternoon when nobody is talking, which is why it is built before the meeting and kept in the front of the notebook.

2 · A colleague says "the visual alphabet has twelve letters, here they are" and lists them. What is the honest response?

The count is a verified fact: XPLANE credits twelve characters to Gray, and Brown's 2011 article says "only twelve forms". The names are not: every fetched source shows the glyphs as an image, and the lists that name them contradict each other. So say twelve, credit Gray, print no list, and teach the five that are indexed by page number. The argument about the number is beside the point, because either way the set is closed.

3 · Which set of properties makes an icon survive six months of meetings?

Every extra element costs a second to draw and a second to decide, so two or three is the ceiling. The recipe fixes the order so the hand stops thinking and a colleague sees the same camera on every page. Thumbnail size is where the icon will actually live, next to capitals. And one per noun, with a letter inside when two nouns collide, is what stops the sleeve and the envelope from being read as each other.
