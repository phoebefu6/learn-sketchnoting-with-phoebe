Part 1 · covers the five minutes before

Prep is five minutes and it decides the page

4 min live

Rohde's strategies chapter puts the work before the event: find out what the talk is about, arrive early, set up the page, have the materials ready. Sessions 2 and 4 turned two of those into specifics you can do in five minutes at the back of a room.

LiveThe live pass: one rule and three habits3 min▶

The rule: capture handles, never sentences. The bench measured what happens when the rule breaks. A handle plus a line in your own words kept all twelve ideas in 110 words; typing along kept ten in 286, with 84 percent of them the speaker's. When you notice yourself writing a fourth word, stop the line and move to the next cell.

Habit one: hear the idea before the sentence ends. Speakers signal. "The big one is..." and "the thing I actually care about..." are the founder telling you which cell to fill. The first three words of a paragraph usually contain the handle.

Habit two: leave the icon blank if it is not in your twenty. Write the handle, draw a small empty box where the mark should go, and move on. You will fill it in the tidy. A new icon invented live costs about twenty seconds, and the bench showed what twenty seconds costs.

Habit three: margin, not shrinking. When the talk overflows the skeleton, and it will, the seventh cell goes in the margin. Making your handwriting smaller to fit is how a page becomes unreadable for the person it is for.

Self-studyThe ten-minute tidy, and why it is not optional4 min▶

Straight after the talk, while the room is emptying and your memory is still loaded, ten minutes on the page: fill the blank icon boxes from your sheet, re-letter the handles in two-line capitals so they carry weight, add one accent to the thing the reader must find first, and write the three or four numbers that were said aloud but did not land on the page. Then photograph it in daylight against a flat surface.

The tidy is where a live capture becomes a work artefact, and it is the step people skip because the talk is over and the page "looks fine". The session 5 test decides it: hand the untidied page to a colleague, ask one question, count. Forty seconds is a private note. Six is something you can send. Nothing between those two numbers is a different page, only a different ten minutes.

Real world

A Daybreak support lead captured the autumn roast talk on a six-cell grid and left three icon boxes empty. In the tidy she filled two from her sheet and found the third was a noun she had never needed before, "origin claim", so she invented a mark for it and added it to the sheet as number twenty-one. That is how the vocabulary grows: at the desk, once, not at speaking pace.

Part 2 · covers the hand-off

The page leaves your desk

4 min live

A photograph of a page is not a recap. The recap is three lines of text in a message with the page attached, and it is what makes the difference between a habit you keep and a habit you abandon in a fortnight.

LiveThree lines, chosen for one reader3 min▶

The recap has exactly three parts and they are always the same three. What it was: subject, length, speaker, page attached, in one line. The three that matter to this reader: not the three biggest, the three that touch their work. Finance gets the margin model, the roastery gets four thousand bags by the first, support gets the FAQ a week early. Same page, three different middles. What happens next: the open question or the thing you will do, which is the line that makes it a message rather than an archive.

Choosing three for a named reader is the same act as choosing twelve from the talk, done once more and harder. If you cannot pick three, the page is fine and you have not decided who it is for.

Self-studyThe one field pattern that exists, and its limits3 min▶

Tidy, Burnham and Elkington (2022) had first-year forensic students sketchnote after each lecture hour and post the pages to a shared board each week, with formative feedback. Students called the practice "Helpful", "Refreshing" and "Creative", about half said they would use it again, and the authors report "a marked increase in the lower grade boundaries" for the group. There was no control group and no effect size, and the authors do not claim one. It is the only fetched study of a sketchnote-plus-sharing routine in the field, and what it supports is narrow: a shared board with feedback kept people doing it.

That is the honest reason the recap is in this session. Not because sending a page makes anyone learn more, which nothing here measured, but because the practices that survive contact with a working week are the ones somebody else is waiting for. Ohmes and colleagues (2024) found the one reliably measured benefit of a sketchnote intervention was affective: negative emotions fell, with no achievement claim attached. A habit you enjoy and somebody expects is a habit that lasts.

Build-along

Capture it live, then send it

★ 26 min · one real talk

The talk you booked, or a twenty-minute recording everyone in the room watches at once. No pausing, no rewinding, and the pens down when the speaker stops.

Five-minute prep. Agenda, item count, pattern, light skeleton, title in two-line capitals with the speaker and the date, icon sheet open beside you. If there is no agenda, draw nothing and plan to work in popcorn for the first two minutes, then rule the page once the shape of the talk is clear.

One live pass. Handles in capitals, one line in your own words, icons from the sheet, blank box where a mark is missing. No sentences, no new icons, no shrinking. If the talk overflows, a margin cell.

Ten-minute tidy. Fill the blank boxes, re-letter the handles, add exactly one accent, and write down every number that was said aloud and is not on the page. Set a timer; at ten minutes the page is finished by definition.

Photograph it. Flat surface, daylight, straight on, no shadow of your phone. Crop to the page edges. This is the artefact; the paper is the backup.

Send the three lines. To a real named person who was not in the room, with the photograph attached. Then score the card below, before they reply.

Sit where you can see the speaker and the screen without turning your head. Turning your head costs a handle every time.

Two pens only: the dark one in your hand, the accent one capped on the table. An accent pen in your hand gets used more than once.

Write the title before the talk starts. A page with a title is a document; a page without one is scrap, and scrap does not get sent.

When you hear a number, write it small under the handle, immediately. Numbers are the one thing memory does not return in the tidy.

At the end, write nothing for thirty seconds. Look at the page and find the one thing the reader must see first. That is where the accent goes, and you cannot know it during.

Send it within the hour. A recap that arrives the next day is competing with the reader's own notes and loses.

If the talk beats you, keep the page anyway. A page with four of twelve ideas and three blank boxes is data: it tells you which habit broke, and it is almost always a decision made during the talk that should have been made before it. The bench showed the same thing with four ideas out of twelve, and the cause there was the same.

Homework

After the course

25 min
  1. Send the recap if you have not. Then note what the reader asked first. It is almost never the thing you put in the accent, and that is the most useful correction you will get.
  2. Capture one talk a week for a month and keep the pages in one place. Month one is where the vocabulary stops growing and the pages start looking like each other.
  3. Run the session 5 test on the fourth page and compare the seconds with your first. One number, four weeks apart, is the only progress measure this practice has.
  4. Add every mark you had to invent to the sheet, at the desk, once. A vocabulary that grows at speaking pace is a vocabulary that costs ideas.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Mid-talk you need an icon for a noun that is not on your sheet. What do you do?

A mark invented at speaking pace costs about twenty seconds, and twenty seconds is the next two ideas. The blank box keeps the place at no cost. The vocabulary grows at the desk, once, where it is also drawn better.

2 · Why does the recap name three things rather than summarising the page?

Finance gets the margin model, the roastery gets the batch date, support gets the FAQ. Same page, three different middles. The whole page is attached for anyone who wants the rest, and selecting for a named reader is what makes the message worth opening.

3 · What do the two field studies of sketchnoting actually support?

Tidy and colleagues report qualitative reactions and "a marked increase in the lower grade boundaries" without a control. Ohmes and colleagues measured emotions and claimed nothing about achievement. The defensible claim is about sustaining the habit, not about memory, which is why the recap is taught as the thing that keeps you doing it.
